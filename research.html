Faster-than-Nyquist Signaling

Research Motivation

Faster-than-Nyquist (FTN) signaling deliberately relaxes temporal orthogonality by transmitting symbols at intervals shorter than the nominal Nyquist spacing. An FTN waveform can be represented as

\[ x(t) = \sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}} d_k\,p(t-k\tau T_s), \]

where \(p(t)\) is the transmit pulse, \(T_s\) is the nominal symbol interval, and \(\tau\) is the temporal packing factor. For \(\tau=1\), symbols are transmitted at the nominal Nyquist rate, whereas \(\tau<1\) increases the symbol density by a factor \(1/\tau\). The price is intentional and structured intersymbol interference (ISI), so waveform design and receiver design become much more tightly coupled than in conventional orthogonal transmission.

My interest in FTN is therefore not limited to using time packing as a direct rate-increase mechanism. I view temporal non-orthogonality as an additional waveform-design degree of freedom. Once orthogonality is relaxed, the pulse shape determines how the desired increase in symbol density is distributed among occupied bandwidth, spectral leakage, alias interactions, ISI structure, numerical stability, and receiver complexity. This viewpoint is particularly relevant to application-specific waveform design and integrated sensing and communication (ISAC), where communication rate is only one of several competing design objectives.

Application-Specific Pulse Shaping for FTN

Pulse shaping plays a fundamentally different role in FTN signaling than in conventional Nyquist transmission. In a Nyquist system, the pulse is commonly designed to satisfy a zero-ISI sampling condition while meeting bandwidth and implementation requirements. In FTN, nonzero ISI is intentional. The relevant question is therefore not whether ISI exists, but how the pulse distributes that ISI and how favorable the resulting channel is for a practical receiver.

After matched filtering and sampling, the discrete-time FTN channel can be written as

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{G}_{\tau}\mathbf{d} + \mathbf{w}, \]

where

\[ [\mathbf{G}_{\tau}]_{m,k} = g\!\left((m-k)\tau T_s\right), \qquad g(t) = \int p(\xi)p^{*}(\xi-t)\,d\xi . \]

Thus, the pulse \(p(t)\) determines the sampled autocorrelation sequence, the Toeplitz FTN Gram operator, and the colored-noise structure after matched filtering. This makes FTN pulse design intrinsically multi-objective. A pulse with low total sampled-ISI energy is not necessarily the easiest pulse to detect, and a globally well-conditioned FTN channel is not necessarily the pulse that gives the best performance under a fixed short-memory detector. Spectral localization, the folded-spectrum floor, total ISI energy, and concentration of the minimum-phase equivalent channel therefore need to be treated as distinct quantities.

Current Work: Cardinal Exponential-Spline Pulses

In my current work, which is under review, I investigate cardinal exponential-spline (CES) pulses as a structured, low-dimensional pulse-shaping family for FTN signaling. Conventional square-root raised-cosine (SRRC) pulses are controlled mainly through a single roll-off parameter. In contrast, the CES family is parameterized by several complex poles,

\[ \boldsymbol{\alpha} = \{\alpha_1,-\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_P,-\alpha_P\}, \qquad \alpha_p=\sigma_p+j\omega_p . \]

The real components \(\sigma_p\) and imaginary components \(\omega_p\) jointly modify the shape of the underlying exponential-spline spectrum and, after cardinalization, the spectral distribution of the resulting pulse. The opposite-sign construction is used to form the pole set; it should not in general be interpreted as complex-conjugate pairing. The final cardinal pulse is obtained through spectral normalization by the integer-shift alias sum of the underlying exponential spline, and is numerically realized over a finite interval for implementation.

The cardinal condition is used here as a deliberate structural constraint, not because FTN fundamentally requires a Nyquist or zero-ISI pulse. Cardinality provides a controlled interpolation structure and a compact parameterization from which FTN pulses can be optimized, but the FTN waveform at \(\tau<1\) remains intentionally non-orthogonal after matched filtering. This distinction is important: the design problem is not to eliminate FTN ISI, but to shape its spectral and numerical properties.

Folded-Spectrum View of FTN Pulse Design

A central quantity in this work is the FTN folded spectrum. It describes the superposition of spectral replicas created by sampling the matched-filter output at the packed symbol rate and can be written as

\[ S_{\mathrm{fold}} (\omega;\boldsymbol{\alpha},\tau) = \frac{1}{\tau} \sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}} \left| \hat{p} \left( \omega+\frac{2\pi k}{\tau T_s} \right) \right|^2 . \]

The folded spectrum is closely related to the spectral symbol of the infinite FTN Gram operator. Consequently, its maximum and minimum provide upper and lower stability bounds. The minimum is particularly important: a deep spectral notch can make the FTN system poorly conditioned even when a peak-to-average ripple measure appears moderate. For this reason, I distinguish folded-spectrum ripple from the condition ratio rather than treating them as interchangeable metrics.

The asymptotic stability measure used in the current design is

\[ \kappa_{\infty} (\boldsymbol{\alpha},\tau) = \frac{ \operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{\omega} S_{\mathrm{fold}} (\omega;\boldsymbol{\alpha},\tau) }{ \operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{\omega} S_{\mathrm{fold}} (\omega;\boldsymbol{\alpha},\tau) } . \]

This direct folded-spectrum quantity is used for optimization and screening. Finite Toeplitz Gram matrices are used only as convergence diagnostics. The distinction is important in strongly packed FTN systems: for the selected \(\tau=0.7\) CES pulse, for example, a \(5\times5\) Gram matrix gives a condition number of only about \(19.8\), whereas increasing the matrix dimension gives approximately \(3120\), \(3956\), \(4250\), and \(4389\) for dimensions \(16\), \(32\), \(64\), and \(128\), respectively. The direct folded-spectrum value is about \(4450\). A small truncated Gram matrix can therefore severely underestimate the actual stability problem.

Multi-Objective FTN Pulse Optimization

In the current CES framework, the pole parameters are selected through an empirical Pareto search. The objectives simultaneously reward spectral efficiency while penalizing energy outside the FTN Nyquist cell and nonuniformity of the folded spectrum:

\[ \underset{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\operatorname{maximize}} \quad \mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \begin{bmatrix} \mathrm{SE}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \\ -\mathrm{OC\!-\!AER}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \\ -\mathrm{Ripple}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \text{subject to} \qquad \kappa_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \leq \kappa_{\max}. \]

The search currently uses six CES poles, corresponding to three opposite-sign pole pairs, with \(\sigma_p\in[-1,1]\) and \(\omega_p\in[-\pi,\pi]\). For each packing factor, 5000 parameter vectors are sampled and screened using hard limits on OC-AER, folded-spectrum ripple, and \(\kappa_{\infty}\). The nondominated candidates form an empirical Pareto set. To avoid selecting a design that gains only a negligible amount of spectral efficiency at the cost of poor conditioning, the final pulse is chosen as the minimum-\(\kappa_{\infty}\) Pareto candidate whose spectral efficiency lies within \(0.5\%\) of the maximum observed Pareto SE.

The resulting tradeoffs depend on the packing factor. For \(\tau=0.6\), the selected CES pulse has gross SE \(5.007\) bit/s/Hz, OC-AER \(-51.83\) dB, folded-spectrum ripple \(2.47\) dB, and \(\kappa_{\infty}\approx1.52\times10^4\). At \(\tau=0.7\), the corresponding values are \(4.297\) bit/s/Hz, \(-51.01\) dB, \(1.82\) dB, and \(\kappa_{\infty}\approx4.45\times10^3\). At \(\tau=0.8\), stronger stability can be obtained (\(\kappa_{\infty}\approx831\)), but the selected CES pulse uses slightly more occupied bandwidth and its gross SE decreases to about \(3.706\) bit/s/Hz. The optimization therefore exposes a tradeoff rather than a single pulse that is uniformly superior in every metric.

Toward Application-Specific FTN Pulses

The broader research direction is to extend this parametric and multi-objective framework beyond communication-centric spectral metrics and design FTN waveforms according to the requirements of the target application. Conceptually, the design can be generalized as

\[ p^{\star}(t) = \underset{p(t),\,\tau}{\operatorname{arg\,min}} \; \sum_{i} w_i J_i\!\left(p,\tau\right), \]

where the objectives \(J_i\) can represent communication efficiency, sensing accuracy, Doppler robustness, spectral masks, receiver memory, implementation cost, or other application-dependent quantities. The current results already show why this extension is necessary. At \(\tau=0.7\), for example, CES has slightly larger total sampled-ISI energy than the bandwidth-matched SRRC pulse, but its minimum-phase equivalent channel is more concentrated within the four taps retained by the reduced-memory detector. Conversely, an SRRC pulse with larger roll-off can achieve even stronger short-memory concentration and lower BER, but only by consuming substantially more bandwidth.

\[ \begin{array}{c} \text{Communication-centric} \\ \Downarrow \\ \{ \mathrm{SE}, \mathrm{OC\!-\!AER}, \mathrm{Ripple}, \kappa_{\infty} \} \end{array} \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad \begin{array}{c} \text{Application-specific} \\ \Downarrow \\ \{ J_{\mathrm{comm}}, J_{\mathrm{sense}}, J_{\mathrm{Doppler}}, J_{\mathrm{complexity}} \} \end{array} \]
Research questions:
  • Can multi-parameter pulse families provide FTN operating points that cannot be reached by varying only the roll-off factor of a conventional Nyquist-derived pulse?
  • Which waveform quantities actually predict performance when the receiver has limited memory: total sampled ISI, the global folded-spectrum condition ratio, minimum-phase channel concentration, or a combination of them?
  • How should FTN pulses be redesigned when communication spectral efficiency is only one objective among sensing accuracy, Doppler tolerance, spectral coexistence, and receiver complexity?
  • Can the pulse parameters, FTN packing factor, and detector memory be jointly optimized rather than treating waveform design and receiver design as separate problems?

Current research: My manuscript under review develops a Pareto-optimized cardinal exponential-spline pulse family for FTN signaling. At \(\tau=0.7\), the selected CES pulse has \(B_{99}=0.9973\) MHz and gross FTN spectral efficiency \(4.2973\) bit/s/Hz, compared with \(1.0010\) MHz and \(4.2815\) bit/s/Hz for an approximately bandwidth-matched SRRC pulse with \(\beta=0.05\). CES reduces OC-AER from \(-39.95\) to \(-51.01\) dB, reduces folded-spectrum ripple from \(2.19\) to \(1.82\) dB, and reduces \(\kappa_{\infty}\) from about \(7805\) to \(4450\). Under the same four-tap, 512-state reduced-memory BCJR receiver, CES also lowers the coded BER: at \(E_b/N_0=10\) dB, the observed BER is approximately \(3.94\times10^{-4}\), compared with \(1.19\times10^{-3}\) for SRRC with \(\beta=0.05\). A larger-roll-off SRRC pulse with \(\beta=0.25\) reaches a still lower BER of about \(2.07\times10^{-4}\), but its occupied bandwidth increases to \(1.1011\) MHz and its gross FTN spectral efficiency decreases to \(3.8923\) bit/s/Hz. This comparison highlights the central design tradeoff: spectral efficiency, global stability, raw ISI, and finite-memory detection performance are related but are not equivalent optimization criteria.

FTN for Sensing-Centric ISAC

In an ISAC system, emphasizing sensing performance can constrain the waveform, bandwidth, power allocation, or precoder and thereby reduce the communication operating space. FTN introduces an additional controllable dimension through the packing factor \(\tau\). Instead of immediately increasing occupied bandwidth, communication throughput can be modified through temporal packing while the pulse is simultaneously shaped to satisfy sensing and receiver constraints.

A sensing-constrained FTN system can be viewed through the optimization

\[ \underset{\tau,\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\operatorname{maximize}} \quad \eta_{\mathrm{c}}(\tau,\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{R_{\mathrm{c}}(\tau,\boldsymbol{\theta})}{B} \] \[ \text{subject to} \qquad J_{\mathrm{s}}(\tau,\boldsymbol{\theta}) \leq \epsilon_{\mathrm{s}}, \qquad 0<\tau\leq1, \qquad P\leq P_{\max}, \]

where \(R_{\mathrm{c}}\) is the achievable communication rate, \(J_{\mathrm{s}}\) is an application-dependent sensing cost, and \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) can include the pulse parameters together with variables such as precoding, power allocation, or receiver settings. In this formulation, \(\tau\) is not simply a rate-control parameter: changing the symbol spacing also changes the temporal correlation, spectrum, ambiguity structure, and interference geometry of the transmitted waveform.

For parameter-estimation applications, sensing requirements may instead be expressed through constraints such as

\[ \operatorname{CRLB}_{r}\leq\epsilon_r, \qquad \operatorname{CRLB}_{v}\leq\epsilon_v, \]

corresponding, for example, to range and radial-velocity estimation accuracy. These constraints create a natural connection between waveform shaping and FTN packing because both affect the temporal and spectral moments that determine sensing information. Another useful characterization is the delay-Doppler ambiguity function,

\[ \chi_s(\Delta,\nu) = \int s(t)s^{*}(t-\Delta) e^{-j2\pi\nu t}\,dt, \]

which characterizes the response of the transmitted waveform to delay and Doppler offsets. FTN modifies \(s(t)\) through both the packing factor and the pulse overlap, so the ambiguity structure cannot in general be inferred from the isolated pulse alone. This motivates studying the complete time-packed waveform rather than optimizing communication and sensing properties independently.

Research questions:
  • How aggressively can symbols be packed while satisfying explicit range, velocity, or ambiguity-function constraints?
  • Can FTN recover communication spectral efficiency when an ISAC waveform is forced toward a sensing-dominant operating point?
  • Can pulse shape and temporal packing be selected jointly so that the resulting non-orthogonality is useful for sensing while remaining manageable for communication detection?

Array and Spatial Signal Processing

Research Motivation

Modern wireless systems increasingly rely on large antenna arrays to exploit spatial degrees of freedom. Accurate estimation of spatial information is essential for applications such as beamforming, localization, multi-user communication, and integrated sensing and communication.

Research Problem

A practical challenge in array processing is that measurements are affected by noise, interference, clutter, and model mismatch. In particular, direction-of-arrival estimation methods based on predefined angular grids may suffer from performance degradation when actual source locations do not align with the assumed grid.

Research Contribution

My M.S. research investigated off-grid direction-of-arrival estimation under measurement noise and heavy clutter conditions. The research focused on improving estimation accuracy and robustness by addressing limitations of conventional sparse recovery approaches.

Sparse and Structured Signal Processing

Research Motivation

Many estimation problems in next-generation wireless systems share a common mathematical structure. A high-dimensional physical quantity must be recovered from a limited number of noisy observations, while only a small number of its components are significant.

A generic sparse inverse problem can be written as

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{n} \]

where \( \mathbf{y} \) is the observation vector, \( \mathbf{A} \) is the sensing or measurement matrix, \( \mathbf{x} \) is the unknown physical representation, and \( \mathbf{n} \) represents noise and modeling errors.

The key assumption is that the unknown vector is sparse:

\[ \|\mathbf{x}\|_0 \ll N \]

meaning that only a small number of entries of \( \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{N} \) contain significant information. This structure can make an otherwise underdetermined estimation problem recoverable.

Although many wireless applications lead to a similar sparse inverse model, how should the recovery algorithm be designed to exploit the specific physical structure of each problem?

Sparse Recovery

Ideally, sparse estimation seeks the solution containing the smallest number of nonzero components:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{x}} \|\mathbf{x}\|_0 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 \leq \varepsilon \]

Since direct \( \ell_0 \)-based optimization is generally combinatorial, practical algorithms exploit alternative formulations. A common convex relaxation uses the \( \ell_1 \)-norm:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{x}} \frac{1}{2} \|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 + \lambda\|\mathbf{x}\|_1 \]

Other possibilities include greedy algorithms, sparse Bayesian learning, message passing, atomic-norm methods, and structured sparse recovery. The appropriate approach depends on the physical meaning of \( \mathbf{A} \) and \( \mathbf{x} \).

A Common Model Across Wireless Applications

The physical meaning of the sparse vector changes across applications, while the underlying inverse problem often remains similar.

Spatial and Direction-of-Arrival Estimation

In array processing, the observation model can be written as

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{ang}} \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ang}} + \mathbf{n} \]

where the columns of \( \mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{ang}} \) represent array responses associated with candidate directions and \( \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ang}} \) contains the corresponding source amplitudes.

\[ \|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ang}}\|_0 \ll N_{\mathrm{ang}} \]

This motivates sparse and super-resolution approaches for direction finding, localization, and spatial sensing.

mmWave and THz Channel Estimation

High-frequency wireless channels are often dominated by a relatively small number of propagation paths. In an angular or beamspace representation,

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{ch}} \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{path}} + \mathbf{n} \]

where \( \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{path}} \) contains the gains associated with possible propagation paths.

\[ L = \|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{path}}\|_0 \ll N_{\mathrm{path}} \]

Sparse recovery can therefore reduce pilot overhead while exploiting the physical propagation structure of large-array systems.

ISAC and Delay-Doppler Recovery

In sensing and integrated sensing and communication, the environment can often be represented by a small number of targets or propagation components in the delay-Doppler domain:

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{DD}} \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{DD}} + \mathbf{n} \]

If only a limited number of delay-Doppler components are present,

\[ \|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{DD}}\|_0 \ll N_{\tau}N_{\nu} \]

sparse estimation can be used for target detection, delay-Doppler estimation, channel sensing, and joint communication-sensing inference.

Grant-Free Massive Access

Sparse models also arise in massive machine-type communication, where a network may contain a very large number of devices but only a small subset are active at a given time.

\[ \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{pilot}} \mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{user}} + \mathbf{N} \]

In this case, sparsity appears in the user-activity domain:

\[ K_{\mathrm{active}} \ll K_{\mathrm{total}} \]

The receiver must determine both which users are active and the information associated with those users.

Near-Field and Extremely Large Arrays

For extremely large antenna arrays, far-field angular models may no longer be sufficient because the channel can depend jointly on angle and range.

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{NF}} \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{AR}} + \mathbf{n} \]

Here, \( \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{AR}} \) represents a sparse angle-range-domain description of the propagation environment. The sparse structure remains useful, but the underlying dictionary and geometry differ from conventional far-field systems.

Same Model, Different Recovery Problems

The applications above share the general model

\[ \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{n}, \qquad \|\mathbf{x}\|_0 \ll N \]

However, the physical structure represented by \( \mathbf{x} \) differs significantly between applications:

  • DOA estimation: sparsity in the angular domain.
  • mmWave and THz channels: sparsity in propagation paths.
  • ISAC: sparsity in delay, Doppler, angle, or target domains.
  • Massive access: sparsity in user activity.
  • Near-field systems: joint angle-range sparsity.

The central challenge is therefore not simply to apply the same sparse algorithm everywhere, but to identify and exploit the structure that corresponds to each physical problem.

Off-Grid and Continuous Parameter Estimation

One important limitation of conventional sparse recovery is the use of predefined discrete dictionaries. For example, an angular dictionary may assume the grid

\[ \Theta_{\mathrm{grid}} = \left\{ \theta_1, \theta_2, \ldots, \theta_G \right\} \]

while a physical direction \( \theta^\star \) can lie between two grid points:

\[ \theta^\star \notin \Theta_{\mathrm{grid}} \]

This basis mismatch can reduce estimation accuracy, particularly in high-resolution array-processing and sensing problems. Off-grid, gridless, sparse Bayesian, and super-resolution methods provide ways to estimate continuous physical parameters more accurately.

Exploiting Structure Beyond Element-Wise Sparsity

Wireless coefficients are often not independent. Significant components may appear in clusters, share common support across antennas or time, or belong to physically related groups.

Suppose the unknown vector is divided into \(G\) groups:

\[ \mathbf{x} = \left[ \mathbf{x}_1^{T}, \mathbf{x}_2^{T}, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_G^{T} \right]^{T} \]

A group-sparse estimator can then use

\[ \min_{\mathbf{x}} \frac{1}{2} \|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 + \lambda \sum_{g=1}^{G} \|\mathbf{x}_g\|_2 \]

rather than penalizing every coefficient independently. Similar ideas can be extended to joint sparsity, hierarchical sparsity, low-rank structure, and tensor representations.

Dynamic Sparse Recovery

Wireless environments are inherently time-varying. In many applications, the objective is therefore to track a sequence of sparse states rather than estimate a single static vector.

\[ \mathbf{y}_n = \mathbf{A}_n\mathbf{x}_n + \mathbf{n}_n \]

The sparse support may evolve gradually:

\[ \operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{x}_n) \approx \operatorname{supp}(\mathbf{x}_{n-1}) \]

Exploiting this temporal relationship can improve channel tracking, beam tracking, target tracking, and dynamic user-activity detection.

Research questions:
  • How should the physical structure of a wireless problem be mapped into the sparse model \( \mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{n} \)?
  • Which recovery method is most suitable when sparsity appears in angle, delay-Doppler, user activity, propagation paths, or angle-range domains?
  • How can off-grid and gridless approaches reduce basis mismatch in continuous angle, delay, Doppler, and range estimation?
  • When should group, joint, hierarchical, low-rank, or dynamic structures be exploited instead of conventional element-wise sparsity?
  • How can sparse recovery remain computationally efficient as the dimensions of massive MIMO, THz, near-field, and ISAC systems grow?
  • How can model-based sparse inference be combined with learning or adaptive methods when the measurement model is imperfect or changes over time?

Research Vision

My interest is not limited to applying a single sparse recovery algorithm across different wireless systems. The broader objective is to identify the physical structure hidden in each inverse problem and develop recovery methods that exploit that structure efficiently.

Learning-Based Wireless Signal Processing

Research Motivation

Classical wireless signal-processing algorithms are usually derived from analytical models of the propagation channel, interference, mobility, noise, and hardware. These models provide valuable physical insight and interpretability, but their accuracy can deteriorate when the actual environment is nonlinear, time-varying, or only partially known.

Learning-based signal processing provides a complementary approach: rather than relying entirely on a predefined analytical model, the system learns useful input-output relationships directly from observed data. I am particularly interested in learning methods that preserve the adaptive and recursive nature of classical signal processing while providing nonlinear modeling capability.

How can learning-based methods exploit observed wireless data while remaining adaptive, computationally efficient, and compatible with the physical structure of communication systems?

From Linear Adaptive Filtering to Kernel Methods

Consider a sequence of input-output observations \( \{\mathbf{x}_n,d_n\} \), where \( \mathbf{x}_n \) contains the available measurements and \( d_n \) denotes the desired response. A conventional linear adaptive filter models the prediction as

\[ \widehat{d}_n = \mathbf{w}_n^{H}\mathbf{x}_n , \]

where \( \mathbf{w}_n \) is updated as new observations become available. This model is computationally attractive, but its representation capability is limited when the relationship between \( \mathbf{x}_n \) and \( d_n \) is strongly nonlinear.

Kernel methods address this limitation by implicitly mapping the input into a higher-dimensional feature space,

\[ \mathbf{x} \longmapsto \boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}), \]

while evaluating inner products in that space through a kernel function

\[ \kappa(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j) = \left\langle \boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_i), \boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_j) \right\rangle . \]

The nonlinear feature mapping therefore does not need to be constructed explicitly.

Example: Gaussian Kernel

A commonly used kernel is the Gaussian radial-basis-function (RBF) kernel,

\[ \kappa(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j) = \exp \left( - \frac{ \left\| \mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j \right\|_2^2 }{ 2\sigma_k^2 } \right), \]

where \( \sigma_k \) is the kernel width. Two observations that are close in the input space produce a kernel value close to one, whereas sufficiently different observations produce a smaller similarity.

The kernel width therefore controls the scale of the nonlinear model: a small \( \sigma_k \) produces a more localized predictor, while a larger \( \sigma_k \) produces a smoother mapping.

Recursive Least Squares

Recursive least squares (RLS) is a classical adaptive filtering technique that updates the model sequentially instead of repeatedly solving a batch least-squares problem. At time \(n\), an exponentially weighted least-squares criterion can be written as

\[ J_n(\mathbf{w}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda^{\,n-i} \left| d_i-\mathbf{w}^{H}\mathbf{x}_i \right|^2 , \]

where \( 0<\lambda\leq1 \) is the forgetting factor. When \( \lambda \) is close to one, the algorithm retains a longer history. Reducing \( \lambda \) gives more importance to recent observations and allows faster tracking of changing environments.

\[ \lambda \approx 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{longer memory}, \] \[ \lambda < 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{faster adaptation}. \]

Kernel Recursive Least Squares

Kernel recursive least squares (KRLS) combines the recursive adaptation of RLS with the nonlinear representation capability of kernel methods. By applying RLS in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS), the resulting predictor can be expressed as

\[ \widehat{d}_n = f_n(\mathbf{x}_n) = \sum_{j=1}^{M_n} \alpha_{j,n}\, \kappa \left( \mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{x}_j \right), \]

where \( \alpha_{j,n} \) are recursively updated coefficients and \( M_n \) is the number of observations represented by the kernel model.

A regularized KRLS formulation can be interpreted through the optimization problem

\[ \underset{f\in\mathcal{H}}{\operatorname{minimize}} \quad \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda^{\,n-i} \left| d_i-f(\mathbf{x}_i) \right|^2 + \mu \|f\|_{\mathcal H}^{2}, \]

where \( \mathcal H \) is the RKHS induced by the selected kernel and \( \mu>0 \) controls regularization.

In this way, KRLS retains the online adaptation mechanism of classical RLS while replacing a purely linear model with a nonlinear kernel-based predictor.

Why KRLS for Wireless Signal Processing?

KRLS is attractive for wireless applications because many relevant processes are simultaneously nonlinear and time-varying. Examples include channel evolution, mobility-dependent observations, interference dynamics, beam prediction, nonlinear receiver processing, and sensing-assisted communication.

The basic learning process can be summarized as

\[ \boxed{ \text{wireless observations} \longrightarrow \text{kernel similarity} \longrightarrow \text{recursive adaptation} \longrightarrow \text{nonlinear prediction} } . \]

In contrast to a model trained once and then kept fixed, a recursive learner can continue updating as the propagation environment evolves. This is particularly useful in systems affected by mobility, non-stationarity, changing interference, or sensing conditions.

Model Growth and Online Complexity

Classical KRLS also introduces an important challenge. If every new observation becomes a kernel basis element, then the model size grows with time,

\[ M_n = n . \]

Consequently, memory consumption and recursive-update complexity can become increasingly expensive in long-running systems.

This motivates sparse extensions of KRLS, where only sufficiently informative observations are retained in the kernel dictionary. Methods based on novelty, coherence, or approximate linear dependency can therefore provide an important extension when KRLS is deployed in real-time wireless systems.

Research directions:
  • How effectively can KRLS track nonlinear wireless processes under mobility and rapidly changing channel conditions?
  • How should kernel parameters and forgetting factors be selected or adapted for different wireless environments?
  • How can sparse dictionary techniques control the computational growth of KRLS without sacrificing prediction performance?
  • How can physical knowledge of channels, antenna arrays, mobility, or sensing systems be incorporated into kernel-based learning?
  • For which wireless problems does online kernel learning provide a better performance-complexity-adaptation trade-off than offline deep learning?

Potential Applications

Kernel adaptive methods can be investigated for a range of wireless signal-processing problems, including channel prediction, adaptive equalization, beam prediction, interference learning, mobility tracking, and integrated sensing and communication.

Research Vision

My broader interest is in learning-based wireless signal processing that combines the interpretability and sequential adaptation of classical signal-processing algorithms with the nonlinear modeling capability of modern machine learning.

Integrated Sensing and Communication

Research Motivation

Integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) enables wireless infrastructure to use the same transmitted signals, spectrum, and antenna resources for both data communication and environmental sensing. One particularly interesting application is predictive beamforming in vehicle-to-infrastructure (V2I) networks, where a roadside unit (RSU) communicates with moving vehicles while simultaneously observing the signals reflected from them.

The reflected sensing signals contain spatial and temporal information about the vehicles. Rather than explicitly estimating intermediate parameters such as angle-of-arrival, angle-of-departure, or channel state information (CSI), this sensing information can be used directly to predict the beamformers required for future communication.

For the \(k\)-th vehicle, we consider the post-processed reflected sensing observation

\[ \bar{\mathbf r}_{k,n} = G\xi\beta_{k,n} \mathbf b(\phi_{k,n},\theta_{k,n}) \mathbf a^{H}(\phi_{k,n},\theta_{k,n}) \mathbf f_{k,n} + \bar{\mathbf z}_{n}, \]

where \(\mathbf f_{k,n}\) is the RSU transmit beamformer, \(\mathbf a(\cdot)\) and \(\mathbf b(\cdot)\) are the transmit and receive array responses of the RSU, \(\beta_{k,n}\) contains the sensing-path characteristics, and \(\bar{\mathbf z}_{n}\) represents measurement noise.

In our work, this post-processed reflected signal is taken directly as the sensing input; the preceding matched-filter and delay-Doppler processing stages are not the focus of the predictive-beamforming problem.

End-to-End Predictive Beamforming

The conventional predictive-beamforming pipeline can be represented as

\[ \text{Sensing signals} \longrightarrow \text{State / CSI estimation} \longrightarrow \text{Beamformer design}. \]

Errors introduced during state estimation propagate into the subsequent beamforming stage. An alternative is to bypass this intermediate representation and learn the mapping

\[ \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{n-1}^{(\tau)} \longrightarrow \{ \mathbf f_{k,n}, \mathbf w_{k,n} \}_{k=1}^{K}, \]

where

\[ \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{n-1}^{(\tau)} = \{ \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{n-\tau}, \ldots, \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{n-1} \}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{n} = \begin{bmatrix} \bar{\mathbf r}_{1,n}^{T}\\ \vdots\\ \bar{\mathbf r}_{K,n}^{T} \end{bmatrix}, \]

contains the reflected sensing observations of all vehicles over multiple previous time slots. The objective is to predict the beamformers for the next time slot directly from these observations.

Communication performance is ultimately determined by the multi-user SINR

\[ \gamma_{k,n} = \frac{ \left| \mathbf w_{k,n}^{H} \mathbf H_{k,n} \mathbf f_{k,n} \right|^{2} }{ \displaystyle \sum_{i\neq k} \left| \mathbf w_{k,n}^{H} \mathbf H_{k,n} \mathbf f_{i,n} \right|^{2} + \sigma_c^{2} }, \]

and the corresponding achievable sum-rate

\[ R_n = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log_{2} \left( 1+\gamma_{k,n} \right). \]

Thus, predictive beamforming is not simply a regression problem: the beamformers of different vehicles are coupled through multi-user interference, and the final objective is communication performance.

Technical Challenge

Existing end-to-end predictive beamforming approaches have demonstrated that deep neural networks can learn the nonlinear mapping from reflected sensing signals to beamforming vectors. However, these models are primarily trained offline using a large collection of channel and mobility realizations.

Vehicular environments are inherently non-stationary. Vehicle motion, changing traffic density, blockage, clutter, channel conditions, and sensing noise can alter the statistical relationship between the reflected observations and the desired beamformers. This motivates an important question:

Can predictive beamforming be learned and continuously adapted online, rather than relying primarily on an offline-trained neural network?

Online Kernel Learning for Predictive Beamforming

Our current research investigates replacing the deep neural network used for end-to-end predictive beamforming with an online approximate-linear-dependency kernel recursive least-squares (ALD-KRLS) framework.

For a network serving \(K\) vehicles, we consider \(K\) vehicle-specific KRLS predictors. Each predictor estimates the beamforming variables associated with one vehicle, while the input can retain the reflected sensing information from all vehicles so that inter-vehicle coupling is not discarded.

\[ \mathbf x_n = \operatorname{vec} \left( \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{n-1}^{(\tau)} \right), \]

and the predictor associated with vehicle \(k\) can be represented as

\[ \widehat{\mathbf q}_{k,n} = \mathcal K_k(\mathbf x_n) = \sum_{j\in\mathcal D_k} \boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k,j} \, \kappa \left( \mathbf x_n, \mathbf x_{k,j} \right), \]

where \(\mathcal D_k\) is the adaptive kernel dictionary, \(\kappa(\cdot,\cdot)\) is the kernel function, and \(\widehat{\mathbf q}_{k,n}\) represents the predicted parameters from which the transmit and receive beamforming vectors of vehicle \(k\) are constructed.

Adaptive Dictionary Growth

A central issue in online kernel methods is that storing every new observation causes the model complexity to grow indefinitely. Approximate linear dependency (ALD) provides a mechanism for adding a new sensing observation only when it contributes sufficiently new information to the kernel dictionary.

For a candidate input \(\mathbf x_n\), its novelty relative to the current dictionary can be measured by

\[ \delta_n = \kappa(\mathbf x_n,\mathbf x_n) - \mathbf k_n^{H} \mathbf K_{\mathcal D}^{-1} \mathbf k_n, \]

where

\[ \mathbf k_n = \begin{bmatrix} \kappa(\mathbf x_n,\mathbf x_1) & \cdots & \kappa(\mathbf x_n,\mathbf x_{|\mathcal D|}) \end{bmatrix}^{T}. \]

The observation is added to the dictionary only when

\[ \delta_n > \delta_{\mathrm{ALD}}, \]

allowing the predictor to adapt to new operating conditions while controlling its memory and computational complexity.

Task-Driven Online Adaptation

An important aspect of this direction is that the goal is not merely to minimize the Euclidean error between predicted and reference beamforming vectors. The ultimate task is to maintain or improve communication performance.

Therefore, the learning problem can be linked directly to the communication utility

\[ \mathcal L_n = - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log_2 \left( 1+ \gamma_{k,n} \left( \mathbf f_{k,n}, \mathbf w_{k,n} \right) \right), \]

so that adaptation is driven by the effect of the predicted beamformers on the end communication objective rather than by beamformer prediction error alone.

Research questions:
  • Can online KRLS achieve predictive beamforming performance comparable to large offline-trained DNNs while requiring substantially less retraining?
  • How quickly can an adaptive kernel predictor track changes in vehicle mobility, channel statistics, sensing noise, clutter, and traffic conditions?
  • How should the kernel dictionary be controlled so that adaptation, prediction accuracy, memory usage, and real-time execution remain balanced?
  • Should each vehicle use only its own reflected sensing history, or should the predictor retain global multi-vehicle sensing information to better account for inter-user interference?
  • Can task-driven kernel updates based directly on achievable sum-rate outperform conventional regression-based beamformer learning?
  • How should confidence or novelty information from an online kernel model be used to decide when the predictor should update, remain unchanged, or request additional information?

Research Vision

More broadly, this research explores whether ISAC-enabled predictive beamforming can evolve from a predominantly offline learning problem toward an adaptive signal-processing framework that learns continuously from the sensing information already available at the RSU.

\[ \boxed{ \text{Reflected sensing} \rightarrow \text{online kernel learning} \rightarrow \text{predictive beamforming} \rightarrow \text{communication utility} } \]

Such a framework combines physical sensing models, adaptive signal processing, kernel learning, and task-oriented beamforming for dynamic ISAC systems.
